Group 1: Text Reviewer

1. Applying the Law of Cosines in Solving Oblique Triangles

An oblique triangle is a triangle without a 90° angle. It can be an acute triangle (all angles < 90°) or an obtuse triangle (one angle > 90°).

Formulas:

  • a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A
  • b² = a² + c² − 2ac cos B
  • c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C

Rule: Uppercase and lowercase letters represent opposite angles and sides (A ↔ a, B ↔ b, C ↔ c).

When to Use:

  • SAS (Side-Angle-Side): Known two sides and the included angle (find missing side).
  • SSS (Side-Side-Side): Known all three sides (find missing angle).

2. Solving Quadratic Inequalities in One Variable

A quadratic inequality in one variable contains a variable raised to the second power (e.g., x² + 3x − 4 > 0). Symbols used are >, <, ≥, and ≤.

Interval Notation:

  • Parentheses ( ): Endpoints are NOT included (used for < or >). Example: x > 3 → (3, ∞)
  • Brackets [ ]: Endpoints ARE included (used for ≤ or ≥). Example: x ≥ 3 → [3, ∞)

3. Solving Quadratic Inequalities in Two Variables

Standard forms: y > ax² + bx + c, y ≥ ax² + bx + c, y < ax² + bx + c, y ≤ ax² + bx + c (a ≠ 0). The solution is an infinite set of ordered pairs (x, y) shown as a shaded region on the Cartesian plane.

4-Step Procedure:

  1. Find Features: Orientation (a > 0 opens up, a < 0 opens down), Vertex h = -b/(2a), x-intercepts (y=0), and y-intercept (0, c).
  2. Draw Boundary: Dashed line for strict (<, >); Solid line for non-strict (≤, ≥).
  3. Test Point: Substitute a point like (0,0) into the inequality to check if it's true or false.
  4. Shade Region: Shade the region containing the test point if true; shade the opposite if false. (y > or y ≥ shade above/inside; y < or y ≤ shade below/outside for upward parabolas).

4. Determining the Region of Solutions (Linear vs. Quadratic)

Evaluates boundary types (solid vs. dashed), half-plane/curve regions (above/below/inside/outside), and overlapping regions for systems of inequalities.

5. Absolute Value Equations in One Variable

Absolute value represents distance from 0. Rule: |x| = a → x = a OR x = -a. (e.g., |x| = 5 → x = 5 or -5).

6. Absolute Value Inequalities in One Variable

  • Less Than (|x| < a): -a < x < a (Between / AND). Example: |x| < 5 → (-5, 5)
  • Greater Than (|x| > a): x < -a OR x > a (Outside / OR). Example: |x| > 5 → (-∞, -5) ∪ (5, ∞)

7. Measures of Position

  • Quartiles (Q): Divide data into 4 equal parts (Q1=25%, Q2=50%/Median, Q3=75%). Formula: Position = k(n + 1)/4
  • Deciles (D): Divide data into 10 equal parts (D1 to D9, D5=Median). Formula: Position = k(n + 1)/10
  • Percentiles (P): Divide data into 100 equal parts (P1 to P99). Formula: Position = k(n + 1)/100
  • Interpolation: For decimal positions: [(Larger - Smaller) × decimal] + Smaller
  • Interquartile Range (IQR): IQR = Q3 - Q1
  • Outliers Rule: Lower fence = Q1 - 1.5(IQR); Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5(IQR). Any value beyond fences is an outlier.

8. Box-and-Whisker Plots & Cumulative Frequency

  • Box-and-Whisker 5-Number Summary: Minimum, Q1, Median (Q2), Q3, Maximum.
  • Cumulative Frequency: Running total of frequencies where the last value equals total frequency (n).
  • Histogram & Polygon (Ogive): Graph showing running totals against class boundaries.

9 & 10. Percentile Rank and Drawing Conclusions

Formula for position: Pk = k(n + 1) / 100. Percentiles measure relative position, not raw percentage scores. Being in the 80th percentile means scoring higher than or equal to ~80% of the comparison group.

Group 2: Questions and Answers

Q1: What is an oblique triangle?
A1: An oblique triangle is any triangle that does not contain a 90° right angle. It can be acute or obtuse.
Q2: What are the three forms of the Law of Cosines?
A2:
a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A
b² = a² + c² − 2ac cos B
c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C
Q3: When should you use the Law of Cosines?
A3: You use it when you are given Side-Angle-Side (SAS) to find a missing side, or Side-Side-Side (SSS) to find a missing angle.
Q4: What is the difference between parentheses ( ) and brackets [ ] in interval notation?
A4: Parentheses mean the endpoint is NOT included (used with < or >). Brackets mean the endpoint IS included (used with ≤ or ≥).
Q5: How do you know whether to draw a solid or dashed curve when graphing quadratic inequalities?
A5: Use a dashed curve for strict inequalities (< or >) and a solid curve for non-strict inequalities (≤ or ≥).
Q6: What is the rule for solving absolute value equations like |x| = a?
A6: Set up two separate equations: x = a OR x = -a.
Q7: How do absolute value inequalities differ between "less than" and "greater than"?
A7: Less than (|x| < a) becomes a compound statement -a < x < a (between). Greater than (|x| > a) breaks into two statements x < -a OR x > a (outside).
Q8: How are quartiles, deciles, and percentiles related regarding the median?
A8: The median is equal to Q2, D5, and P50.
Q9: How do you calculate outliers using the 1.5 × IQR rule?
A9: First find IQR (Q3 - Q1). Values below (Q1 - 1.5 × IQR) or above (Q3 + 1.5 × IQR) are outliers.
Q10: Does scoring at the 80th percentile mean you scored 80% on a test?
A10: No. It means your score was equal to or higher than 80% of the people who took the test.

Group 3: Real-Life Examples

1. Applying Law of Cosines

Real-Life Scenario: Navigation and Surveying. A ship sails from a port 10 miles due East, then turns 45° and sails another 15 miles. To find the direct distance from the port back to the ship without a right triangle, navigators use the Law of Cosines (SAS setup).

2. Solving Quadratic Inequalities in One Variable

Real-Life Scenario: Business Profit Margins. A business determines that its daily profit P relative to product price x follows a quadratic curve. They solve P(x) ≥ 0 to find the price range where the company turns a profit rather than operating at a loss.

3 & 4. Quadratic Inequalities in Two Variables & Solution Regions

Real-Life Scenario: Civil Engineering & Safe Zones. Determining the safe structural boundary of a suspension bridge cable curve. Engineers shade the region under the parabolic cable path (y ≤ ax² + bx + c) to specify where support beams can safely be placed.

5 & 6. Absolute Value Equations and Inequalities

Real-Life Scenario: Quality Control & Manufacturing Tolerances. A machine fills bag chips targeting 100 grams, but allows an error margin of 5 grams. This is written as |x - 100| ≤ 5. Acceptable bags range between 95g and 105g; anything outside this interval is rejected.

7. Measures of Position (Quartiles, Deciles, Percentiles)

Real-Life Scenario: Pediatric Growth Charts. Doctors measure a toddler's height and weight and report that they are in the 75th percentile (P75). This informs parents that their child is taller than 75% of kids their age.

8. Box-and-Whisker Plots & Cumulative Frequency

Real-Life Scenario: Real Estate Analysis. Real estate agents use box plots to summarize housing prices in a neighborhood, showing buyers the minimum price, maximum price, and the middle 50% (IQR) of home values without getting distorted by extreme outliers.

9 & 10. Percentile Rank and Statistical Conclusions

Real-Life Scenario: Standardized Testing (e.g., SAT / College Entrance Exams). A student receives a percentile rank of 90 on a national entrance exam. The university concludes that this applicant performed better than 90% of all national test-takers, qualifying them for admission.
